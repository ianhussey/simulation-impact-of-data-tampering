# Fanelli (2009) — How Many Scientists Fabricate and Falsify Research? A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Survey Data

**Citekey:** `fanelli_how_2009` · *PLOS ONE* 4(5):e5738 · doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0005738 · **PDF:** `files/38851/`

The first meta-analysis of misconduct surveys and still the most-cited. 21 surveys reviewed, 18 meta-analysed. Deliberately restricted to behaviours that *distort scientific knowledge* — fabrication, falsification, "cooking" — excluding plagiarism and other professional misconduct.

## Headline pooled estimates

| Behaviour | N | Pooled | 95% CI |
|---|---|---|---|
| Self-admitted fabrication/falsification/data modification, ≥1× | 7 | **1.97%** | 0.86–4.45 |
| — restricted to questions using the words "fabrication"/"falsification" | 4 | 1.06% | 0.31–3.51 |
| Self-admitted other QRPs | 20 | up to **33.7%** (crude mean 9.54%) | — |
| Colleague observed to have falsified | 12 | **14.12%** | 9.91–19.72 |
| Colleague observed doing other QRPs | — | up to **72%** | — |

Meta-regression: self-report surveys, surveys using the explicit words "falsification"/"fabrication", and mailed surveys all yield *lower* rates. Medical/pharmacological researchers report more once these are controlled. Fanelli's own conclusion is that these are conservative estimates.

## Why this matters for the manuscript

Two distinct uses.

**1. The prevalence asymmetry.** ~2% admit fabrication/falsification; up to 33.7% admit other QRPs; the observed-behaviour figures are 14% and up to 72%. Same argument as Xie et al. (2021), on the older and better-known evidence base. Cite both.

**2. The conceptual passage on the falsification continuum (p. 1)** — this is the most useful prose in the paper for our purposes and is worth reading before writing the Discussion:

> There can be little doubt about the fraudulent nature of fabrication, but falsification is a more problematic category. Scientific results can be distorted in several ways, which can often be very subtle and/or elude researchers' conscious control. Data, for example, can be "cooked" … it can be "mined" … it can be selectively published only when it supports one's expectations … Depending on factors specific to each case, these misbehaviours lie somewhere on a continuum between scientific fraud, bias, and simple carelessness.

And: "Ultimately, it is impossible to draw clear boundaries for scientific misconduct … However, the intention to deceive is a key element."

This is the continuum our simulations traverse, stated by the field's most-cited prevalence researcher in 2009. Our contribution is to put a *number* on the distance between its ends: the effort in tampered data points is nearly the same at both.

Note also the Babbage (1830) definition of "cooking" quoted by Fanelli — "an art of various forms, the object of which is to give to ordinary observations the appearance and character of those of the highest degree of accuracy" — which is a rather good 195-year-old description of a greedy tampering algorithm.

## Suggested use

- **Introduction**: cite alongside Xie et al. (2021) for the prevalence asymmetry; Fanelli for the original synthesis, Xie for the update.
- **Discussion / ethics**: the continuum passage. It establishes that the field already knows the boundary is blurry; the manuscript's move is to quantify how blurry, in units of effort.
- Possibly a footnote on the meta-regression finding that using the *words* "fabrication"/"falsification" halves admission rates (1.97% → 1.06%) — direct evidence that the labelling, not the behaviour, drives what people will admit to. That is close to the manuscript's thesis stated as a measurement artefact.

## Caution

Small k for the headline self-admission estimate (N = 7 questions) with wide CIs and significant heterogeneity. The "up to 33.7%" and "up to 72%" figures are maxima across questions, not pooled estimates — quote them as ranges, not as point estimates.
