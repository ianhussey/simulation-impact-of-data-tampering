# Entradas, Feng & Carneiro e Sousa (2026) — The 'shades of grey' in research integrity: Researchers admit to questionable research practices that they do not perceive to be serious

**Citekey:** `entradas_shades_2026` · *PLOS ONE* 21(1):e0339056 · doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0339056 · **PDF:** `files/38849/`

Survey of researchers in Portuguese universities across six fields (n = 1,573 of 9,002 contacted; 17.4% response). Twelve QRPs, each rated both for **engagement** and for **perceived seriousness** (1–4 index), with regression models predicting engagement.

## Key findings

- The average researcher admits **4.3 of 12** QRPs (SD 2.5, median 4). **91%** report at least one; **32%** report six or more; only 9% report none.
- QRPs are equally frequent across gender, seniority level, and field of research. More-published and younger researchers admit more.
- Perceived seriousness varies 2.8–3.9 on the 1–4 index. Most serious: using another's idea without credit, failing to disclose conflicts, research without ethical approval. Least serious: citing publications because they are visible, gift authorship, **developing hypotheses after seeing the results**.
- **Perceived seriousness is by far the strongest predictor of engagement (B = −0.353, p < .001)**, dwarfing number of publications (B = 0.164), age (B = −0.095) and field (B = 0.052).

## Why this matters for the manuscript

**This supplies the causal mechanism the manuscript's argument depends on.** The manuscript claims that treating exclusion as venial and fabrication as unthinkable risks *miscalibrating prevalence*. Entradas et al. show why that is not merely a measurement worry: how serious a practice is perceived to be is the dominant predictor of whether researchers actually do it, well ahead of every demographic and career factor measured.

So the perception gap documented by John et al. (2012) — data exclusion rated 1.61/2 for defensibility, falsifying data 0.16/2 — is not an inert attitude. If perceived seriousness drives engagement, then the fact that our simulations find exclusion and fabrication to be equally cheap and equally damaging means the field's seriousness ratings are systematically mis-set on exactly the dimension that governs behaviour.

The title phrase is also directly usable: our strategies live in the "shades of grey" between RCR and FFP, and our contribution is to show that the grey zone is, in effort and in evidential damage, not grey at all.

## Suggested use

- **Discussion / ethics section**, as the second step of the argument: (i) John et al. and Geggie show the perception gap; (ii) our simulations show the gap is unjustified on effort and bias grounds; (iii) **Entradas et al. show perception drives behaviour** — so the unjustified gap is not merely an intellectual inconsistency but a live cause of the behaviour.
- Optionally in the **Introduction** for the "91% report at least one QRP" figure as evidence that the grey zone is near-universally occupied.

## Caution

Low response rate (17.4%), single country, self-report, cross-sectional — the seriousness→engagement relation is correlational and could as easily run the other way (people who do a thing rationalize it as not serious). State it as an association, and note the reverse-causation possibility if the point is load-bearing. The twelve QRPs surveyed are mostly reporting- and authorship-related and do **not** include data exclusion or fabrication, so this paper supplies the mechanism, not the specific rates — pair it with John et al. (2012) for those.
