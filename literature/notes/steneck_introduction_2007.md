# Steneck (2007) — Introduction to the Responsible Conduct of Research

**Citekey:** `steneck_introduction_2007` · **PDF:** `files/38852/` (184 pp; the relevant material is Chapter 2, pp. 19–22)

ORI's RCR handbook. The source of the US Federal Research Misconduct Policy definitions as they are taught, and the source of the RCR/QRP/FFP three-way taxonomy that later prevalence work (Xie et al., 2021) organizes itself around.

## The definitions (Federal Research Misconduct Policy, quoted p. 21)

> Research misconduct is defined as fabrication, falsification, or plagiarism in proposing, performing, or reviewing research, or in reporting research results.
>
> - **Fabrication** is making up data or results and recording or reporting them.
> - **Falsification** is manipulating research materials, equipment, or processes, or changing or omitting data or results such that the research is not accurately represented in the research record.
> - **Plagiarism** is the appropriation of another person's ideas, processes, results, or words without giving appropriate credit.
> - Research misconduct does not include differences of opinion.

To count as misconduct, an action must additionally (p. 20): represent a "significant departure from accepted practices"; have been "committed intentionally, or knowingly, or recklessly"; and be "proven by a preponderance of evidence."

Steneck is explicit that this is a floor, not a ceiling (p. 21): the definition "establishes a minimum standard for measuring acceptable behavior, not a standard for judging all research behavior. In particular, it does not imply that all other behaviors are acceptable."

## Why this matters for the manuscript

**This is the taxonomy our strategies straddle, and the reason the straddle is the point.** Map the simulated strategies onto the federal definitions:

| Strategy | Operation | Federal category |
|---|---|---|
| Fabrication, duplication | **adds** data points that were never collected | *Fabrication* — "making up data or results and recording them" |
| Dropping | **omits** genuine observations | *Falsification* — "omitting data or results" |
| Condition switching, re-pairing | **changes** labels/pairings without adding or deleting | *Falsification* — "changing … data or results" |
| Replacement | **deletes and adds** in one operation | Both |

The federal taxonomy classifies by *mechanism*: did you invent it, or did you alter/omit it? Our results show that mechanism is close to irrelevant to both the effort required and the harm done. Every strategy produces the same thing — "results that did not exist before" — at a similar cost in tampered data points, and with a manufactured effect of near-identical size. The distinction that carries the moral and regulatory weight (invent vs. omit) is orthogonal to the distinction that carries the evidential weight (how much did the research record move, and how cheaply).

Note also that the second clause of *falsification* — "such that the research is not accurately represented in the research record" — is a **consequence** criterion, not a mechanism one. It is the clause that unifies all six strategies. That is the sentence to lean on: every strategy here, adding or removing, satisfies it identically.

## Suggested use

- **Introduction**, when first drawing the QRP/fabrication continuum: cite for the formal definitions rather than paraphrasing, so the later argument that the boundary is arbitrary is made against the authoritative wording rather than a straw version.
- **Discussion / ethics section**: the central rhetorical move. Our adding-strategies are textbook *fabrication*; our removing-strategies are textbook *falsification*; the effort estimates say they are interchangeable. Deleting data and inventing data are the same act measured in keystrokes and in damage to the record, and only the federal taxonomy — not the evidence — treats them as different kinds of thing.
- Also the origin of the QRP-vs-FFP framing used by Xie et al. (2021) and Gopalakrishna et al. (2022); worth one clause noting the taxonomies are shared so the prevalence estimates are commensurable.

## Caution

This is a 2007 ORI handbook, not a peer-reviewed article; cite it as the authoritative statement of the *definitions*, not as evidence about prevalence or about how researchers actually behave. Steneck's own framing (a minimum standard, silent on everything else) is worth preserving — it is what makes room for the QRP grey zone that our simulations traverse.
