# Wang, Yan & Katz (2018) — Researcher Requests for Inappropriate Analysis and Reporting: A U.S. Survey of Consulting Biostatisticians

**Citekey:** `wang_researcher_2018` · *Annals of Internal Medicine* 169(8):554–558 · doi:10.7326/M18-1230 · **PDF:** `files/38821/`

390 consulting biostatisticians (74.7% completion) drawn at random from ASA membership, asked how often over the past five years they were **directly asked** to do each of 18 inappropriate things, and how severe they considered each request. Funded by ORI.

## The items that matter here (Table 1)

| Request | Rated "most severe" | Asked 0× | 1–9× | >10× |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Falsify the statistical significance (e.g. the *P* value) to support a desired result | 84% | 97 | 2 | 1 |
| Change data to achieve the desired outcome | 84% | 93 | 7 | – |
| **Remove or alter some data records (observations) to better support the research hypothesis** | **80%** | **76** | **22** | **2** |
| Interpret findings on the basis of expectations, not actual results | 68% | 70 | 28 | 2 |
| Do not report key missing data that could bias results | 68% | 76 | 23 | 1 |
| Ignore violations of assumptions because results may change to negative | 64% | 71 | 28 | 1 |
| Remove categories of a variable to report more favorable results | 48% | 68 | 31 | 1 |

**24% of biostatisticians had been asked to remove or alter data records** at least once in five years — versus 7% asked to change data outright and 3% asked to falsify significance directly. Overall, 54–84% of every demographic subgroup reported receiving at least one of the eight highest-severity requests.

## Why this matters for the manuscript

This is the **demand side**, and it reproduces the manuscript's asymmetry from a third angle. The three requests are, in our terms:

- "Remove or alter some data records" ≈ our **dropping** / **replacement** strategies — **24%**
- "Change data to achieve the desired outcome" ≈ our **fabrication** / **condition switching** — **7%**
- "Falsify the statistical significance" ≈ skipping the tampering and editing the *p* value — **3%**

Same ordering, same rough magnitudes as the self-report literature (Xie 2021; John 2012): the closer a request comes to being nameable as fabrication, the less often it is made — even though, by our results, "remove some data records" and "change data" require a comparable number of edits to manufacture the same significant result. Note that all three are rated "most severe" by 80–84% of respondents, so the severity judgement here does *not* track the request frequency; what varies is what researchers are willing to ask for out loud.

Distinctively, this is not a self-report of one's own misconduct but a report of **requests received**, which sidesteps the social-desirability problem that limits John et al. and Fanelli — the respondent is not the one confessing.

## Suggested use

- **Introduction**, one clause establishing that this is not a hypothetical: roughly a quarter of consulting biostatisticians have been asked to remove or alter observations to support a hypothesis. It converts the simulation from an abstract exercise into a model of a documented request.
- **Discussion**, alongside the FDA/avacopan case (`us_fda_proposal_2026`) as the two pieces of real-world grounding — one showing the request is common, the other showing what happens when it is granted.
- Note that younger biostatisticians reported significantly higher frequencies on 6 of 8 items; consistent with Gopalakrishna et al. (2022) on career stage.

## Caution

Biomedical/biostatistical consulting only. Frequencies are of *requests received*, not of requests granted — the paper is explicit that it has no data on how the requests were handled or whether they reflected maleficence or simply poor methods training. Do not write this up as a prevalence of misconduct.
