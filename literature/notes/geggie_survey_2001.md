# Geggie (2001) — A survey of newly appointed consultants' attitudes towards research fraud

**Citekey:** `geggie_survey_2001` · *Journal of Medical Ethics* 27(5):344–346 · doi:10.1136/jme.27.5.344 · **PDF:** `files/38842/`

Questionnaire to medical consultants appointed 1995–2000 across seven Mersey-region NHS trusts. 194 replies from 305 (63.6%). Small, old, and single-region — but it contains the cleanest within-subject demonstration of the fabricate-vs-omit asymmetry in the whole collection.

## The key items (Table 1)

Respondents were asked what they would be *willing to do*, under two different incentives:

| Item | Yes | Unsure | No |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5A. If it would expedite publication, would you **fabricate data**? | **0** | 3 | 191 |
| 5B. If it would expedite publication, would you **omit data to fit your hypothesis**? | **3** | 17 | 174 |
| 6A. If it would enhance a grant application, would you **fabricate data**? | **1** | 3 | 189 |
| 6B. If it would enhance a grant application, would you **select or omit data to fit your hypothesis**? | **8** | 19 | 167 |

Derived variables: observed misconduct 55.7%; past personal misconduct 5.7%; willing or unsure about future misconduct 18.0%. Only 17% had ever received training in research ethics.

## Why this matters for the manuscript

**The 5A/5B and 6A/6B pairs are a matched-pairs experiment on the manuscript's exact question.** Same respondents, same hypothetical incentive, same response scale — the only thing that varies is whether the act is described as *inventing* data or *omitting* data:

- Willing-or-unsure to **fabricate**: 3/194 (publication), 4/194 (grant)
- Willing-or-unsure to **omit**: 20/194 (publication), 27/194 (grant)

A **5–7× difference in stated willingness**, for two acts that our simulations show cost a comparable number of tampered data points and produce a comparable manufactured effect. Because the comparison is within-subject and the two items sit adjacent on the same page of the same questionnaire, the difference is attributable to the framing of the act and essentially nothing else.

This complements John et al. (2012) nicely: John shows the asymmetry in *what people have done* and *how defensible they think it is*; Geggie shows it in *what people say they would be willing to do*. Different populations (UK medical consultants vs US psychologists), different decades, same asymmetry.

## Suggested use

- **Introduction or Discussion**, immediately after or alongside John et al. (2012). Two independent literatures, two decades apart, showing the same thing — that is more persuasive than either alone.
- The strongest single sentence available from this paper: willingness to omit inconvenient data runs roughly five to seven times higher than willingness to invent it, in the same respondents answering adjacent questions.

## Caution

Very small numbers in the cells that matter (0, 1, 3, 8 "yes" responses out of 194) — the ratios are unstable and should be described qualitatively ("several times higher") rather than as a precise multiple. Single UK region, 2001, medical consultants; do not generalize the *levels*, only the *contrast*. Also note this measures stated willingness, not behaviour, and social desirability presumably suppresses the fabrication item more than the omission item — which is itself the point, but means the gap in behaviour may be smaller than the gap in stated willingness.
