# Gardner, Lidz & Hartwig (2005) — Authors' reports about research integrity problems in clinical trials

**Citekey:** `gardner_authors_2005` · *Contemporary Clinical Trials* 26(2):244–251 · doi:10.1016/j.cct.2004.11.013 · **PDF:** `files/38839/`

Mail survey of 549 authors of pharmaceutical clinical trial reports published 1998–2001 (indexed in the Cochrane Database), asking about fabrication or misrepresentation in three contexts. 64% replied among those with valid addresses.

## Key findings

| Context | Rate |
|---|---|
| The *target article* (the specific paper the author was contacted about) misrepresented the research | **1%** (2 authors) |
| Fabrication or misrepresentation in a study the author **participated in** in the last 10 years | **~5%** |
| Personally knows of a case of fabrication or misrepresentation in the last 10 years | **17%** |

The authors' own conclusion: "Fraud and misrepresentation in clinical trials appear to be rare on a per-published report basis. However, they occur sufficiently frequently that scientists have a significant chance of participating in a project affected by fraud or misrepresentation during their research careers."

## Why this matters for the manuscript

**This is the per-article denominator, and it is the framing that makes the manuscript's effort estimates consequential.**

Most prevalence figures in this collection are *per researcher* ("have you ever…"). Gardner et al. supply the complementary quantity: roughly 1% of published trial reports are said by their own authors to misrepresent the research, while ~5% of researchers have participated in such a study and 17% know of one. The gap between the per-article rate and the per-career rate is the useful structure: a low per-paper rate is compatible with a high probability that any given researcher encounters fraud, because papers are numerous.

For our purposes: if manufacturing a false positive costs only a few per cent of the sample — and our results say it does, regardless of sample size — then the constraint on how much of this happens is not *difficulty*. It is willingness, opportunity, and detection. Gardner et al. show that willingness and opportunity are not rare at the career level even where the per-article rate looks reassuringly small.

Note also that Gardner et al. explicitly discuss the definitional problem, quoting the same federal FFP definitions as Steneck (2007), and observe that "fabrication of results falls easily within" the definition while misrepresentation of research is harder to classify — the same boundary problem the manuscript exploits.

## Suggested use

- **Introduction or Discussion**, one sentence, to establish scale in per-article rather than per-researcher terms and to pre-empt the "but fraud is rare" objection: rare per paper, not rare per career.
- Useful as a counterweight so the manuscript does not appear to claim that fabrication is rampant. The claim is about *cost*, not *frequency* — Gardner et al. let us state the frequency honestly (low per report) while the simulations state the cost (low per manufactured result).

## Caution

Self-report by authors about their own papers, which is the most socially-desirability-suppressed configuration possible — the 1% target-article figure is a floor and the authors say so. Pharmaceutical clinical trials only, 1998–2001. The three rates measure different things and should not be presented as a single trend.
