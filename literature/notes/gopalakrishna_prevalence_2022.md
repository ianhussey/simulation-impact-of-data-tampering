# Gopalakrishna et al. (2022) — Prevalence of questionable research practices, research misconduct and their potential explanatory factors: A survey among academic researchers in The Netherlands

**Citekey:** `gopalakrishna_prevalence_2022` · *PLOS ONE* 17(2):e0263023 · doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0263023 · **PDF:** `files/38847/`

The National Survey on Research Integrity: 6,813 respondents across all disciplinary fields and ranks in the Netherlands. Methodologically the strongest prevalence study in the collection — it uses the **randomized response method** for the fabrication and falsification items, which gives respondents provable deniability and so removes much of the social-desirability suppression that limits ordinary self-report.

## Headline estimates

| Behaviour | Prevalence | 95% CI |
|---|---|---|
| **Fabrication** ("making up of data or results") | **4.3%** | 2.9–5.7 |
| **Falsification** ("manipulating research materials, data or results") | **4.2%** | 2.8–5.6 |
| Any FF | 8.3% | 6.2–10.3 |
| Frequently engaging in ≥1 of 11 QRPs | **51.3%** | 50.1–52.5 |

Most prevalent QRPs: not submitting valid negative studies (17.5%), insufficient reporting of flaws and limitations (17.0%), insufficient supervision (15.0%), insufficient attention to equipment/skills (14.7%), inadequate note-taking (14.5%), selective citation (14.0%).

Predictors: publication pressure raises QRP engagement (OR 1.22); scientific norm subscription (OR 0.79) and perceived likelihood of detection by reviewers (OR 0.62) lower misconduct. PhD candidates/junior researchers and men engage more.

## Why this matters for the manuscript

**The fabrication and falsification rates are essentially identical (4.3% vs 4.2%) — and both are about twice the usual self-report estimates.** Two things follow.

1. When you remove the social-desirability barrier via randomized response, the gap between inventing data and altering/omitting data *closes almost completely*. Compare Xie et al. (2021), where ordinary self-report gives 1.9% fabrication vs 3.3% falsification, and John et al. (2012), where fabrication is admitted at 0.6–1.7%. This is direct evidence that the apparent rarity of fabrication relative to other data-tampering is substantially a **reporting artefact**, not a behavioural fact — which is exactly the manuscript's claim, arrived at from the opposite direction (we show the acts cost the same; they show that people admit to them at the same rate once it is safe to do so).

2. Because our simulations show adding and removing data points require comparable effort, the near-equality of these two rates is what one should *expect*. That is a satisfying convergence to note in the Discussion.

Also usable: **perceived likelihood of detection by reviewers is protective (OR 0.62)**. Our distribution-preserving strategies (condition switching, re-pairing) are precisely the ones that no univariate check by a reviewer can detect. If detectability deters, then the cheapest strategies in our results are also the least deterred ones.

## Suggested use

- **Introduction / Discussion**: the 4.3% vs 4.2% equality, framed as "when asked under conditions that make honest answering safe, researchers admit to fabrication and falsification at indistinguishable rates."
- **Discussion**, connecting to the detectability limitation: cite the detection-likelihood OR when noting that adding a detectability axis is the most valuable extension of our work — deterrence appears to operate through perceived detectability, and our stealthiest strategies are undetectable by construction.
- The publication-pressure OR is available if a sentence on incentives is wanted, though it is not central.

## Caution

Netherlands-only; randomized response gives wide CIs and requires strong assumptions about respondent compliance; the QRP items are Likert-frequency based ("score 5, 6 or 7") rather than binary, so the 51.3% is not comparable to binary admission rates elsewhere. The fabrication/falsification equality is the durable point — do not over-read the small numerical difference in either direction.
